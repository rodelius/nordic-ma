I recommend a composable corporate-event model: a common lifecycle, event-specific milestones, parallel workstreams and party-specific financial exposures. A single list of transaction stages will lose important information.

I have created an extensive reference model covering 40 event templates, 526 milestones and 24 linked data entities.

1. Separate transaction type from transaction structure

Several of your examples describe different aspects of the same transaction.
Dimension	Examples
Economic objective	Acquisition, disposal, capital raising, refinancing, restructuring
Execution route	Bilateral negotiation, auction, public offer, scheme, IPO, court process
Ownership outcome	Minority investment, joint control, majority acquisition, full ownership
Financing structure	LBO, corporate funding, bridge financing, project finance
Separation method	Carve-out, spin-off, split-off, asset transfer
Programme	Take-private, dual-track exit, strategic alternatives review


For example, a sponsor-led take-private might contain a public offer, an LBO financing package, a carve-out and subsequent bond refinancing. These should be linked records, with controls against counting the acquisition value or cash flows several times.

2. Use a common lifecycle, with independent workstreams

The common lifecycle provides a consistent overview:

Origination → Strategic review → Preparation → Engagement → Evaluation → Negotiation and selection → Commitment → Conditions and execution → Completion → Post-completion

Alongside this, record separate states for:
Workstream	Illustrative progression
Due diligence	Scoping → Access granted → In progress → Issues identified → Substantially complete → Complete
Documentation	Drafting → Negotiating → Agreed form → Executed → Effective
Financing	Capacity assessed → Indicative terms → Credit approved → Committed → Available → Drawn → Refinanced
Regulatory	Applicability assessed → Pre-notification → Filed → Review → Remedies → Clearance or prohibition
Shareholders	Documentation → Meeting or election → Voting/acceptance → Result
Separation	Perimeter defined → Design → Implementation → Day-one ready → Legal separation → TSA exit
Hedging	Exposure identified → Validated → Strategy reviewed → Executable → Executed → Monitored → Settled
Closing	Checklist open → Funds flow agreed → Conditions confirmed → Ready → Funded → Settled → Reconciled


These are typical progressions, not mandatory sequences. Each authority should have its own approval record: one clearance does not establish that all regulatory conditions are fulfilled. 4. Refine the acquisition stages in your example

Your suggested stages are useful, but they mix evidence, activities and legally significant milestones.
Your proposed label	Recommended treatment
Rumoured	Evidence classification attached to a claim
Planning	Lifecycle phase
Non-disclosure signed	Dated milestone, specific to a party/pursuit
Indication of intent	Distinguish expression of interest, indicative offer and non-binding IOI
Letter of intent	Document with separately recorded binding and non-binding provisions
Exclusivity	Agreement with scope, start, expiry and termination provisions
Data room access	Access permission; not proof of completed diligence
Quality of earnings	Financial diligence deliverable, possibly vendor- or buyer-commissioned
Drafting SPA	Documentation workstream state
Final binding bid	Bid record with validity, reservations and conditions
Signing SPA	Executed agreement milestone
Approvals	Individual conditions and authority decisions
Closing	Event-specific legal/economic completion, supported by evidence


In an auction, final bids often precede exclusivity. In a bilateral deal, exclusivity may precede confirmatory diligence. SPA drafting, diligence and financing commonly overlap.

5. The underlying data model

The principal relationships should be:
Entity	Purpose
Programme	Groups related events and alternative routes
Corporate event	Defines the bounded ownership, capital or liability change
Event relationship	Records “finances,” “requires,” “alternative to,” “separates” and similar links
Entity/event party	Identifies buyers, sellers, targets, borrowers, investors and advisers
Transaction perimeter	Defines included/excluded businesses, assets and liabilities
Pursuit/bid	Preserves each bidder’s separate progress and offer revisions
Workstream	Tracks parallel activity
Milestone occurrence	Records what happened, when and with what evidence
Condition/approval case	Tracks gates, authorities, deadlines, decisions and remedies
Funding instrument	Separates committed, available and drawn financing
Economic leg	Records a specific payment, receipt, contribution or distribution
Risk exposure/hedge	Connects economic risks to named parties and actual contracts
Source/claim	Preserves evidence, uncertainty and conflicting assertions
Transition/feedback	Maintains history and reviewed corrections
Forecast/opportunity	Separates predictions and commercial actions from established facts


Every material fact should carry an effective date, observation date, source, evidence status and revision history.

6. The FICC layer should begin with economic legs

Headline transaction value is insufficient to identify a hedging need.

For each payment or receipt, capture:
- Who: payer, recipient and economically exposed entity.
- What: purchase price, debt draw, repayment, fees, tax, dividend, capex or earn-out.
- How much: currency, amount/range and gross/net basis.
- When: settlement window, draw schedule and maturity.
- Under what conditions: signing, closing, acceptance, approvals or other triggers.
- What offsets it: accessible cash, same-currency financing and existing hedges.
A EUR-based sponsor buying a Swedish target for SEK consideration may need to acquire SEK. A EUR-based selling shareholder receiving SEK may need the opposite conversion. These are separate exposures and opportunities.

The model must also distinguish:

Evidence confidence ≠ probability of completion ≠ commercial opportunity score.

Likewise, financing committed ≠ financing drawn; IPO trading ≠ proceeds received; offer settlement ≠ squeeze-out completed; carve-out readiness ≠ disposal completed.

Those distinctions are what make the model useful for both Corporate Finance process monitoring and timely FICC advice.
